Timeline of the tanks

The Troubled History of Tank Farms in South Portland

The first tank farms were built in South Portland in the 1940s to support fuel movement during World War II, sited within long-established neighborhoods, and alongside businesses, farms, and schools. We’ve learned more about the consequences of those decisions.

The EPA investigates tank emissions:

2011: The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency begins investigating tank farms in New England, specifically looking at emissions from heated tanks.

2012-2013: The EPA requires two companies in South Portland, Global and Sprague, to actually test the emissions from their heated tanks. When the results are in, the EPA issues notices of violation to the oil companies for breaching state permits and the Clean Air Act.

2019: After the Maine Department of Environmental Protection (DEP) fails—over years—to act, the EPA sues both operators, arguing they are "major" emitters that should be licensed as such.

The news breaks. The city reacts:

2019: The lawsuits create a public outcry but ends in consent decrees with Sprague and Global. These include minimal fines and installation of vapor-control technology but no binding requirements for monitoring or reducing hazardous air pollutants.

In July, Protect South Portland hosts a standing-room only community meeting for residents to share concerns about the tanks, the fumes and the potential health effects of exposure.

2020: The EPA sues Gulf Oil (owner of a tank farm near Bug Light) for violations in its emissions permits. That resulted in a civil penalty of $2.4 million.

The Legislature directs DEP to study the best ways to measure and control tank emissions (SP 662 / LD 1915), but DEP's response recommends little beyond the status quo.

South Portland partners with the Maine DEP to use new and existing ambient air quality monitoring stations to track air quality around the city.

  • The state refuses to place monitors near tanks embedded in residential neighborhoods.
  • The monitor nearest to tanks—but not adjacent to homes most affected by them—is at Bug Light. When the DEP presents its health analysis of the monitoring data, the Bug Light data finds evidence of “burst emissions,” created when tankers come in and offload their product.
  • Residents' calls for action are met with a response to wait for more testing.

The push for more information:

2021: Protect South Portland and 11 other advocacy groups ask the Legislature to require continuous monitoring along the tank fenceline and twice-yearly testing of heated tanks.

2022: The city creates a Clean Air Advisory Committee to draft recommendations for future action.

2023: The city's Clean Air Advisory Committee issues its final report, recommending:

  • Air monitoring stations located closer to the tank farms.
  • Steps to better understand potential burst emissions.
  • Advocacy for regulatory change.

A bill (LD 163/LD 71) that includes advocates’ demands—fenceline monitoring and testing of the heated tanks—is signed into law.

Fenceline monitoring begins - with alarming results:

2024: Fenceline data collection begins.

2025: The first year of raw data from fenceline testing is released.

2026: DEP's own analysis of that data confirms that benzene at some fencelines, specifically Citgo (Mechanic Street) and South Portland Terminal (Lincoln Street), regularly and dramatically exceeds Maine’s ambient air guideline for long-term exposure. Benzene is a known human carcinogen.

Fenceline findings, point by point

Fenceline Monitoring High Level Findings

10 of the 11 facilities statewide had at least one fenceline monitoring location where the long-term average benzene concentration exceeded Maine’s Ambient Air Guideline (AAG) which is the state’s health-based benchmark for evaluating long-term (chronic) exposure risk. Portland Pipe Line (PPL) South Portland was the only facility in compliance at every fenceline monitor. The tanks at PPL are largely empty.

Worst violators:

  • South Portland Terminal LLC (Buckeye Partners), near Lincoln Street and Forest City Cemetery. Its worst-performing monitors averaged as high as 4.5 times the AAG, the highest exceedance in the state.
  • CITGO (South Portland, Mechanic Street/Turner Island area): At Citgo, all 18 fenceline monitors showed benzene above the AAG, meaning elevated levels ring the entire facility perimeter, including points directly adjacent to homes in the Pleasantdale neighborhood. The average on CITGO’s highest single monitor reached roughly 2.3 times the AAG.

At most other exceeding facilities (Global, Sprague South Portland, Sunoco, Irving/Searsport, Sprague/Searsport, Penobscot Bay, Cold Brook, Buckeye/Bangor), exceedances were generally no more than 2.5 times the AAG, and typically limited to just one or two monitoring locations on the fenceline rather than facility-wide.

Toluene and xylenes were below AAGs at every facility. Ethylbenzene exceeded AAG only slightly, and only at one monitor each at CITGO, South Portland Terminal, and Buckeye (Bangor).

DEP pointed to no risk from short term, acute-level exposures. However, the residents of South Portland are experiencing long-term exposures at levels that violate long-term exposure guidelines and are known to be detrimental to human health.

FAQs from the Oct. 15 community meeting

Questions posed by community members at the community meeting on October 15, 2026.

Timeline

  • Why the hell is this taking so long? The 1940s until 2011 is a long time. Do decision-makers actually need more data to act? No. We don’t think we need any more data to act. The DEP has said it wants to see how far these high benzene readings travel beyond the fenceline, but there are houses that sit immediately adjacent to the fenceline. It’s time to do something.

Power

  • Who (what entity/entities) can regulate the emissions from the oil tanks? The Maine Department of Environmental Protection is the licensing entity for the tank farms, issuing new permits and renewals, subject to state regulations. It is responsible for enforcing the federal Clean Air Act in Maine.
  • How do we disallow the emissions that are allowed to be released every year? Who can force corporations to behave as more responsible community members? The Maine DEP is the key regulatory entity, issuing permits with emissions limits. The state Legislature has a role to play in shaping those regulations. And the courts have played a role in holding emitters accountable when they violate emissions standards.

Because this issue is so centered in South Portland, the Maine DEP has said, at various times, that they are following the lead of city officials. That means we all have a role in pushing the council to take a strong stance and to demand action, not just more testing.

Toxins

  • What’s in the tanks? What’s emitted into the air? The truth is, we don’t really know what is emitted. Each tank farm may have a variety of petroleum products stored in the tanks, including gasoline (for cars), heated asphalt, fuel oil. We can guess at what is emitted—and some local experts have done this, using estimates based on substances and how much product tank operators report moving through these facilities. Without direct testing, we can’t say for sure.

    We know the emissions contain a variety of Volatile Organic Compounds, including Hazardous Air Pollutants like benzene and naphthalene. Unfortunately, the fenceline monitors were not able to accurately measure naphthalene emissions. They did measure benzene, and found high levels at tank farms across South Portland, particularly Citgo in Pleasantdale and South Portland Terminal in Ligonia/Forest City Cemetery area.

  • I understand that many of the tanks are empty. Is there a time limit on this? Like I couldn’t keep a junk car in my yard for very long. As long as a tank farm operator does not violate their operating permit, they may renew indefinitely, under current law.
  • What is the cumulative impact of the sites, not just an analysis on a permit-by-permit basis? This is a key question that DEP has never really addressed. The tanks are cumulatively licensed to emit approximately 600 tons of VOCs, which is about what a modest oil refinery would emit. Imagine if we had an oil refinery sitting at the edge of Casco Bay—how much attention would its licensing process receive? Instead, the tanks are licensed separately, and their operations are far less visible. There is precedent for emitters to be licensed within a “source aggregation” framework, where individual licenses are considered within the cumulative impact. That’s not used here, but it’s something we think the city could press for.

Economics

  • What are the benefits of having the tanks in the community? Is the benefit of the tanks spread widely in Maine, while the costs are concentrated in our community? The benefits to the city are quite limited. The tanks do not pay significant property tax and are not major local employers. The benefits to the state are great. About half of the petroleum used in Maine enters through Portland and South Portland.
  • How does the City of South Portland benefit from inaction? One consideration is that the city is reluctant to face a lawsuit. The city took strong action in 2014, passing the Clear Skies Ordinance to block Portland Pipeline from reversing its pipeline to Canada, to bring tar sands oil from Montreal, and building a processing facility on the waterfront. Portland Pipeline sued. The city prevailed, but the proceedings cost the city about $2.8 million.
  • What do we need to change in order to not need tanks? Move our economy away from extractive fossil fuels. This is something we can work on at every level of the government. If you’re interested in advocating for better public transportation and real bike and pedestrian access in South Portland and the region, let us know—we will connect you with people working on that right now.

Models to follow

  • What have other communities done successfully in similar situations to improve emissions? There are good examples of communities succeeding in getting tank farms shut down, mostly through lawsuits. The Conservation Law Foundation succeeded in pressuring ExxonMobil to shutter its tank farm in Everett, Mass., and is working on a similar case in Providence. Earthjustice also has had success stopping or slowing tank farm expansions. Members of Protect South Portland have tried to get both groups, but especially CLF which has an office in Maine, to take up this case, without success. We would welcome community support in engaging environmental justice organizations.
  • Is there a gold standard internationally on ideal emissions and emissions mitigation/control? Technology exists to control emissions at these facilities. The tank farms are not going to invest in it voluntarily.

Political Questions

  • Which candidates for City Council this year are committed to action on this issue? We don’t yet have a good answer to this.

What the community wants to do about this:

Create Vision & Strategy

  • Define our end goal: Do we want to completely stop the emissions? Do we tamp them down? Do we have the tanks moved? Where do the tanks go? Everyone in this community should be able to open their windows without fear that they are letting in toxic pollutants that could harm themselves or their children. Everyone should feel confident that the air in their neighborhood is safe, that they can enjoy Bug Light or the Greenbelt without being exposed to an emissions burst. That their children can breathe clean air while running around the playground at their school or daycare. Removing the tanks altogether is a clear end goal, and one that will take time. In the meantime, the tank farms must clean up their dirty operations. Allowing them to continue to operate as is, knowing what we now know, would be a dereliction of duty on the part of the DEP.
  • What levers of power are the most impactful for us to leverage? Local? State? Federal? Under a different administration, and given that the U.S. EPA has intervened in the past, one could say all three are important. Today, the state is key and local officials must loudly and clearly push for the state to do more.

Build Power

  • How do we work with our political allies to push for state legislation or city ordinances that aligns with our vision and goals? Packing the room on Oct. 27 and getting the city council to take a strong stance moving is key. We need their voice to make the strongest possible case to state leaders.
  • How do we make getting rid of the tanks so eye-catching and motivating that people rally around it and politicians are inspired to take real action? This is a great question and we welcome your input! One thing to consider is that this is NOT JUST AN AIR ISSUE. Most of these tanks are old and sit within flood zones. If they fail, they will devastate Casco Bay. If they fail and catch fire, our air quality troubles will be much, much bigger. They are a massive safety hazard to this community. And, the removal of the tanks presents an incredible opportunity to reimagine the South Portland waterfront.
  • How do we get the DEP to shift from working with corporations to working with us? What would make the state care more? This is a big question that’s really about shifting the structure of our economy and governmental systems. The tanks issue is part of a bigger demand for making corporate power accountable to the people all across the state.

Explore levers to enact change

  • How do we make South Portland inhospitable to this kind of industry without changing state/federal legislation? Another great question. There are some local options. There is precedent in Maine for municipalities creating their own local environmental protection office to license polluters in their jurisdictions. Jay, Maine, did this successfully to regulate the International Paper Co. mill there. South Portland could do this if it had the funding and the will. Also, there is a movement within the state to allow municipalities to create industry-specific tax rates. That could be a tool here.
  • How can we see the emissions data in real time? You can’t. The fenceline data is collected in two-week periods then reported out annually as averages. Real-time data is something we can advocate for.

Letter to the editor guidance

Letters to the Editor - Points for Writing - 150 words maximum

The Press Herald’s letters to the editor page

The council plans a Dec. 1 workshop on options like licensing fees for oil companies, mandating better emissions-control technology, and working with state legislators on statewide policy. Letters that run before then can shape what’s on the table. In addition to reiterating the core message, pick one or two points below and craft a letter.

1. Core message

Residents are suffering and there is ample data of unacceptable levels of emissions from existing testing. Ongoing monitoring is not mitigation. Mayor Elyse Tipton said that testing without further action is not acceptable for the community. We residents should hold the council to that, with binding rules.

Protect South Portland and our neighbors have two goals:

  • End harmful exposure to VOCs from the tanks.
  • Address and account for past and current harm the tanks have caused and continue to cause to public health.

We have been meeting with city councilors and state legislators on a plan with three elements: financial accountability, community education, and regulatory oversight.

2. DEP has had years to act

  • Nearly a decade ago, DEP declined to act against Global Partners because the company’s own data showed no violation. EPA used a different methodology, issued violation notices for years, and sued.
  • DEP now recommends another year of data collection, and DEP and the Maine CDC are launching a one-year program to place sensors even closer to some residences. More information is welcome, but it is not a substitute for controls. In addition, there are already residences right up against the fenceline so we already know what’s happening in residents’ backyards.
  • DEP’s own air quality division director acknowledged that other petroleum storage facilities in Maine aren’t this close to residential neighborhoods. This is a South Portland problem and needs a South Portland answer.

3. A track record that has earned skepticism

  • Operators’ self-reported numbers were contradicted by independent testing once already. Residents are right not to take them on faith again.
  • Whatever was true in the past, no operator can claim ignorance now. Fenceline data, a CDC report, and years of resident complaints have put them on notice.

4. Citgo: promises aren’t protections

  • Citgo announced its emissions plan the day after a packed council meeting, more than a month after the CDC analysis, and nearly a year after the first fenceline monitor readings were made public.
  • Citgo pledges more advanced detection and monitoring, faster infrastructure improvements, and a review of operating practices. The coverage describes no specific controls, reduction targets, deadlines, or independent verification. It sounds like a nice talking point to give the impression of cooperation, but there are no specifics.
  • The raw data shows that fenceline monitors at Citgo’s terminal regularly logged long-term average benzene above state guidelines. In DEP’s report, all 18 Citgo fenceline monitors were above the ambient air guideline meant to be protective of human health.
  • Voluntary promises aren’t enough. Residents need enforceable mitigation, with deadlines, public reporting, and consequences for missing them
  • Voluntary pledges can be revised or dropped. Only enforceable requirements with public reporting protect neighbors: trust, but codify.
  • We need a solution that applies to all tank farms, not just Citgo.

5. Benzene and the people most at risk

  • Benzene is a known human carcinogen linked to leukemia, and the WHO says no safe exposure level has been identified.
  • Children breathe more air relative to body weight and their blood-forming systems are still developing. Older adults and people with weakened immune systems may be less able to tolerate the damage.
  • The state toxicologist said officials are not concerned about short-term exposures in the 1-to-14-day window. However, the concern is the years-long exposure to these levels of benzene - which is happening to those at fenceline in South Portland. “Not acutely dangerous” is not the same as “safe.”
  • Personal stories work best. Several residents shared theirs at the Oct 27 city council meeting, such as asthma, headaches, and preschool-age kids smelling the tanks and not being allowed to go outside on certain days. Use your own story if you have one.

6. Hold the city to its word

What can the city and state do, and what can we do as a community? Ask for action on all three elements of our plan.

Regulatory oversight

  • Local ordinance or licensing: The council should adopt binding requirements that cut tank emissions using proven, available technology, with independent monitoring to verify the result. South Portland’s Clear Skies Ordinance survived six years of litigation, so local action has precedent here.
  • State legislation and DEP licensing: Press legislators and DEP to make these standards apply statewide. Emissions control technology exists and is one of the options that Massachusetts requires certain types of storage tanks adhere to. Massachusetts also uses a benzene guideline about 13 times stricter than Maine’s. The burden should be on operators to prove their emissions are safe, not on residents to prove harm.
  • Potential to Emit (PTE): PTE is the maximum emissions a facility could release, and it decides whether the facility is regulated as a “major” or “minor” source and which controls apply. Those figures have leaned on operators’ own formula-based estimates, as the Global case showed. Ask for measured, verified emissions and a fresh PTE review in light of the fenceline data.

Financial accountability

  • Operators should pay for oversight: The council’s Dec. 1 workshop is set to consider licensing fees for oil companies. Ask that any fees fund independent monitoring and enforcement, so residents and taxpayers are not left covering the cost.
  • Account for the harm already done: Ask the council, legislators, and the city’s counsel to explore how operators can be made to bear the costs of the health impacts these tanks have caused and continue to cause, such as health assessments and follow-up for fenceline neighbors.

Community education

  • Publish the next workshop date(s) and agendas: Ask the mayor to keep putting tank emissions on the agenda regularly and report out publicly until a solution is in place.
  • Make the data public and understandable: Ask DEP, the Maine CDC, and the city to publish fenceline and neighborhood monitoring results in plain language, so residents, schools, and child-care providers can see what is in the air and what it means.
  • Protect residents and help them protect themselves: Ask DEP to use its full authority to keep residents safe from tank fumes, and ask the Maine CDC to publish clear, practical guidance on steps households can take at home to reduce exposure and on symptoms that residents and their doctors should watch for.
  • What residents can do: Read the Tank Farms Toolkit, attend the Dec. 1 workshop and other public meetings, submit public comments, and share your own story.

7. Closing

  • “We have the data, the technology, and the precedent. What’s left is the will.”
  • “Our children shouldn’t be the monitoring program.”
  • “Stop studying the problem and start solving it.”
